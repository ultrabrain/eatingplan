Why this is cardiac medicine, not just joint pain

A 2022 JAMA study followed 62,574 gout patients with 10,475 cardiovascular events and asked whether heart attacks and strokes cluster after flares. They do. In the 60 days following a gout flare, the risk of MI or stroke was roughly doubled — odds ratio 1.93, and still elevated at 1.57 in days 61–120.

The important design detail: they used a self-controlled analysis, where each patient is compared against himself at other times. That means it cannot be explained by your lipids, your blood pressure, your genetics or any other fixed trait. Separately, people under 45 with gout have more than double the cardiovascular disease risk of same-age peers — and that's the age band you're in.

Run your own numbers. Two flares in 2026 means two 60-day windows at roughly doubled risk — about 4 months of the year — plus two further 60-day windows at OR 1.57, taking it to roughly two-thirds of the year with some elevation. That's with a calcium score of 91 and an LDL of 5.2 in the background. Fewer flares than I said last time, but this is still not a trivial share of your life. Preventing them isn't about comfort. On this evidence it's plausibly cardiovascular prevention. Honest caveat: this is observational, and no trial has yet proven that suppressing flares reduces cardiac events.

Note also that the >2× cardiovascular risk in under-45s with gout is about having gout at all — not about how often you flare. That part doesn't move with frequency.

What to ask for — in order

1
HLA-B*58:01 blood test — before any allopurinol, no exceptions
This is the item most likely to be skipped, and the one with the worst downside. Allopurinol can cause a severe skin reaction (SJS/TEN/DRESS) with mortality up to 30%, and this gene variant raises the odds enormously (pooled OR ~118). Malaysian carriage rates are high: 21.8% Chinese, 12.6% Indian, 11.9% Malay — well above the figures the American guideline was written against. Malaysian national data show 2.5 severe reactions per 1,000 new allopurinol users.

Taiwan screened 2,910 people prospectively, diverted the carriers, and got zero severe reactions against 7 expected. The catch: screening is not routine, not mandatory and not subsidised in Malaysia — it costs around RM250 out of pocket and only some labs offer it. Thailand, Korea and Hong Kong fund it universally; Malaysia doesn't. Ask for it by name. RM250 once, before a drug you may take for thirty years.
2
Allopurinol — not febuxostat
ACR strongly recommends allopurinol as first-line, starting at ≤100 mg/day and titrating up to target. Febuxostat matters to avoid specifically because of your calcium score. The CARES trial found higher cardiovascular death on febuxostat than allopurinol (HR 1.34), the FDA added a boxed warning in 2019 and restricted it to people who've failed allopurinol, and ACR conditionally recommends switching away from it in anyone with cardiovascular disease. In fairness, the later FAST trial found no such harm and was methodologically stronger — but it excluded the sickest cardiac patients, so it doesn't fully settle it. Either way allopurinol is first-line, so the debate only matters if you can't take it.
3
Colchicine cover for the first 3–6 months
Also a strong recommendation. Starting allopurinol triggers flares at first — lowering urate destabilises existing crystal deposits and sheds them into the joint. Skipping this is the single most common reason people quit treatment in month two and conclude it didn't work. Typical: colchicine 0.5 mg daily alongside the allopurinol, for 3–6 months.

A quiet bonus: 0.5 mg daily is exactly the dose used in the cardiovascular colchicine trials (LoDoCo2: 31% fewer cardiac events in stable coronary disease; FDA-approved for that purpose in 2023). Whether to continue it past 6 months for heart reasons is a real but genuinely uncertain question — those trials enrolled people with prior heart attacks, not calcium scores. Park that decision until after your CT angiogram, which will tell you which population you actually belong to.
4
Stop reaching for NSAIDs when a flare hits — especially diclofenac
The European Society of Cardiology position is blunt: non-aspirin NSAIDs should generally not be used in people with established or high cardiovascular risk, and "no safe treatment window exists" — even short courses. Diclofenac raises major vascular events by 41% and is the one most commonly handed out for gout in Malaysia. They also push blood pressure up, and your diastolic is already 95.

Use instead: low-dose colchicine at the first twinge (1 mg then 0.5 mg an hour later — most effective within 12–24 hours of onset, so keep a supply at home and when travelling); or a steroid injection into the joint if it's a single joint, which avoids systemic effects entirely; or a short oral prednisolone burst. If an NSAID is truly unavoidable, naproxen ≤500 mg is the least bad — never diclofenac.

Why the flare came after a 30 km run

Four things stack up on a long hard run, and Malaysian heat amplifies all of them:

  • ATP breakdown during intense effort generates urate directly — an internal purine load, no food involved.
  • Lactate competes with urate at the same kidney transporter (URAT1), so you excrete less of it while you're running.
  • Sweat losses concentrate it — 30 km in Malaysian humidity is a lot of fluid.
  • The rebound. Urate typically drops below baseline during recovery. Flares cluster around change in either direction, not around any particular level — which is exactly why starting allopurinol needs colchicine cover.

Evidence grade here is low — the trigger data comes from a survey where only 37.5% of patients could identify any trigger at all, with "excess physical activity" and "dehydration" each named by 4.9%. But early-onset gout, before 50, was significantly more likely to have identifiable triggers. That's you.

Running with gout — practical rules: hydrate deliberately rather than by thirst on long runs · never pause allopurinol around races or travel (stopping causes a rebound, which is itself a trigger) · don't start or increase allopurinol in the 2–4 weeks before a target race — time titration to an easy training block · carry colchicine and take it at the first prodrome · skip the post-run beer and the ikan bilis. Don't cut your training volume — there's no evidence that helps gout, and the cardiovascular benefit of your running clearly outweighs the modest trigger signal.
And don't crash-diet. Fasting, keto, very-low-carb and fasted long runs all generate ketones, which compete with urate for the same kidney exit and sharply cut excretion — the classic starvation-triggered gout mechanism, documented since 1965. Bariatric surgery data show exactly this: urate rises and flares spike in the first month of rapid loss before improving long-term. At BMI 25.4 you have little to lose anyway. Keep it to ~0.4 kg/week, no intermittent fasting, and eat carbohydrate around long sessions.

What this means for the food advice below

Be clear-eyed about this: diet is no longer your urate strategy. The ACR guideline says plainly that "dietary modifications likely yield only small changes in serum urate." The best diet ever tested in a randomised trial — DASH — moved urate by 0.25 mg/dL. A single beer moves it 0.16. Whole-of-diet scoring explains ≤0.3% of the variation in serum urate between people; genetics explains 23.9%. You need to come down about 2.4 mg/dL. Diet delivers roughly a tenth of that. Allopurinol delivers 2–3 mg/dL.

What the food advice is still for: your LDL of 5.2, your HbA1c of 5.8%, your blood pressure — where it genuinely works — and for avoiding acute flare triggers, which is a different thing from lowering the baseline. Alcohol, fructose and a big purine load can provoke an attack even when they barely move your steady-state number. So the ikan bilis and sugary drinks advice stands. Just don't expect it to fix the underlying level.
The plan

Seven rules, in order of how much they buy you

If you only ever do three things, do 1, 2 and 3.

1
Drink your calories to zero
A standard teh tarik is ~23 g sugar per 250 ml glass — 5 to 6 teaspoons. Kopi is ~19 g. Two a day is ~40 g of sugar, roughly 300 kcal, and liquid fructose is the one dietary input that generates uric acid rather than just delivering it. Sugary drinks: 33% higher odds of hyperuricaemia, 21% higher odds of gout. This single change hits weight, HbA1c and urate simultaneously. "Kurang manis" is not enough — most of the sugar lives inside the sweetened condensed milk, which the uncle doesn't adjust when he reduces the spooned sugar. You need kosong, or change the milk (teh-C uses evaporated, not condensed).
2
Get to 75 kg — then reassess
That's −5.6 kg (7%), the threshold used by the Diabetes Prevention Program (58% risk reduction) and the Malaysian T2DM CPG. The ReTUNE trial — the only one run in non-obese people, mean BMI 24.8, almost exactly you — found a median 6.5% loss achieved diabetes remission in 70% of participants. Aim ~0.4 kg/week. Your InBody's 73 kg target is beyond where the evidence stops, and losing 7.6 kg of pure fat would put you near 15% body fat — an athlete composition, not a health target. Treat 73 kg as a conditional stretch goal only if HbA1c hasn't normalised at 75 kg. Don't crash-diet: fasting raises uric acid by 1.1–2.4 mg/dL, and under-fuelling marathon training is its own problem.
3
Don't drink the soup
Every Malaysian dish measured above 2,000 mg sodium per serving is a bowl of soup or gravy noodles. Soto is 3,169 mg — 158% of your entire daily limit in one bowl. Malaysian men already average 3,519 mg/day against a 2,000 mg target, and cooked food bought away from home is the main vector. Order kuah asing, eat the noodles, leave the broth. Cutting ~1,725 mg/day is worth about −5.4/−2.8 mmHg.
4
Santan and deep-fried are the saturated-fat problem — not eggs, not coconut water
Your budget is roughly 17 g saturated fat/day (7% of ~2,200 kcal). One roti john is 18 g; one bowl of curry laksa is 17.8 g. Head-to-head, coconut oil raises LDL more than palm oil (+0.53 mmol/L) and more than non-tropical oils (+0.27). Swapping ~7% of energy from saturated to polyunsaturated fat predicts about −0.36 mmol/L LDL. At home: soybean, canola, sunflower, corn, olive or peanut oil instead of palm/coconut. Santan becomes occasional, not daily.
5
Vegetables and protein before the rice — then walk 10 minutes
In people with prediabetes, eating vegetables and protein first with a 10-minute gap cut the glucose area-under-curve by 38.8% and the peak by over 40%. Free, no food changes needed. Then a 10-minute walk after each meal lowers postprandial glucose 12% on average and 22% after dinner — and that trial matched total walking volume, so the benefit came purely from timing. You already run; just move some of it to after meals.
6
Fix the potassium deficit — it's bigger than the sodium excess
Malaysian adults average 37 mmol potassium/day (~1,450 mg) against a 3,510 mg WHO target — 99% fall short. Raising potassium is worth about −5.3/−3.1 mmHg in hypertensive adults. Local sources are cheap and everywhere: bayam, kangkung, pucuk paku, sweet potato and its leaves, labu, tomatoes, dhal, chickpeas, tempeh, papaya, guava, watermelon, banana. Pisang berangan has a measured GI of 55 — rare in being both high-potassium and low-GI.
7
Stack the four LDL foods daily
The "Portfolio diet" combination lowers LDL ~17% in trials, ~13–14% free-living. Components with their own evidence: plant sterols (biggest single add-on — an extra 8–9% on top of a statin, equal to doubling the dose), viscous fibre ≥3 g β-glucan (−0.25 mmol/L), nuts 28–42 g (−0.12 mmol/L), soy protein ~25 g (−3.2%). What you do not need to restrict — and this surprises people

Tofu, tempeh, dhal, lentils, chickpeas, fresh mushrooms, bayam, kangkung, asparagus, kacang. Despite containing purines, plant sources show no association with gout in any cohort tested — Choi's 47,150-man study found purine-rich vegetables at RR 0.96 (ns) while meat was 1.41 and seafood 1.51. A meta-analysis found them inversely associated (OR 0.86). Soy is protective in Singapore Chinese data (HR 0.86). Feeding trials with tofu moved urate by 0.02 mg/dL.

Eggs: zero purine, and the LDL effect is real but small (~0.05–0.12 mmol/L per 100 mg cholesterol). Moderate to 3–4/week as a precaution given LDL 5.2, but they're not your problem.

Milk and yoghurt: actively helpful. Total dairy RR 0.56 for gout; low-fat dairy 0.58. Milk is uricosuric — a crossover trial showed ~10% urate reduction over 3 hours. Low-fat, for the saturated fat.

Coffee: keep it. Just kosong.

Daily

The LDL stack — three things, every day

On top of Crestor + Ezetrol. Revised 14 Aug: plant sterol milk is out — see below.

Drop the plant sterol milk. Your ezetimibe already does that job. Ezetimibe blocks NPC1L1, the intestinal sterol transporter — it's literally the drug used to treat sitosterolemia, and it cuts serum plant sterol levels by ~50%. Plant sterols in food work one step upstream, by crowding cholesterol out of micelles, so there's some mechanistic room for both. But the human evidence is three small trials and they disagree: the only blinded one (Jakulj 2005, n=40) found adding 2 g of plant sterols to ezetimibe bought −3.5% LDL, p=0.13 — not significant. The two positive trials were unblinded or uncontrolled-feeding, n=21 and n=41. Realistic incremental benefit: somewhere between 0 and 7%, on a base your drugs will already have cut roughly in half.

And the products are sweetened milk powders. Adding a daily sweetened drink to chase maybe 0.06–0.1 mmol/L, when your HbA1c is 5.8%, is a bad trade. If you want the last few percent of LDL, that's a rosuvastatin dose conversation with your cardiologist — not a tin of milk powder.

Your post-run headaches

You mentioned these are much better with hydration, but still appear after hard 25–30 km sessions. Most likely this is benign. One possibility needs ruling out, and one action item is urgent regardless.

Do not take a triptan or an ergotamine — ever — unless a cardiologist clears it. This is the single most actionable thing on this page. Sumatriptan (Imigran) and ergotamine (Cafergot) are both readily available in Malaysia and a busy GP hearing "I get migraines after running" may reasonably reach for one. They are vasoconstrictors, and the FDA label lists ischaemic coronary artery disease as an absolute contraindication. A documented case: a 50-year-old received one subcutaneous sumatriptan dose and developed 10/10 chest pain with inferior ST elevation within minutes — on angiography she had only a 30% non-flow-limiting lesion. Mild plaque is enough.

This contraindication attaches to your coronary calcification, not to your headache diagnosis — it applies whether or not the headaches turn out to be cardiac. Put it on your phone's medical ID and say it out loud to any doctor or pharmacist who offers you something for a "migraine."

The thing to ask about: cardiac cephalalgia

It's a headache caused by myocardial ischaemia — angina that refers to the head instead of the chest. It's in ICHD-3 as code 10.6. Two facts make it worth naming out loud in your case:

  • In consecutive clinical series, roughly 1 in 4 cases present with headache and no chest pain at all.
  • Every patient in the largest series (n=30) had at least one cardiovascular risk factor. You have four. It's the one feature that's essentially universal.
Being straight about the odds: this is probably not what you have. Cardiac cephalalgia is rare, the median patient is around 64 years old, 87% are over 50, and your headaches have recurred over a long period rather than appearing as one acute event. Primary exercise headache — a benign entity affecting ~12% of the population and common in runners — is far more likely. But the asymmetry matters. If it's benign and you investigate: minor cost, a reassuring answer. If it's cardiac and you don't — or you get handed a triptan — that's a different category of outcome. You have a CT coronary angiogram booked in a month. This costs you one sentence at that appointment.

Your dehydration theory is the weakest-evidenced explanation on the table

Not wrong — but not established either, and worth knowing that. Dehydration headache has no dedicated ICHD-3 code (it falls into the 10.7 catch-all). The intervention literature amounts to three small unblinded trials — you cannot blind someone to how much water they drink — and IV fluids for acute headache in the emergency department have not been shown to improve pain outcomes. "More water helped" is an uncontrolled n-of-1 observation that coincided with other changes in your training. It's a reasonable hypothesis; it shouldn't be a settled conclusion.

There's an opposite problem that looks identical: exercise-associated hyponatraemia. Too much plain water, not too little. Headache is its cardinal symptom. In the Boston Marathon study (n=488), 13% of finishers had sodium ≤135 mmol/L; the dominant risk factors were weight gain during the race (OR 4.2) and duration over 4 hours (OR 7.4). It has killed at least 14 athletes since 1981. Critically: the treatments are opposite. Drinking more for what is actually hyponatraemia makes it worse, and giving isotonic fluid to a symptomatic EAH patient can be fatal.

Your 25–30 km sessions are probably under 4 hours, which puts you below the strongest risk threshold, and you're male and experienced — both lower-risk. So this is possible but not likely. It becomes worth taking seriously if you're deliberately front-loading large volumes of plain water.

Three cheap things that would settle most of this

1
Weigh yourself immediately before and after every long run
Free, takes 20 seconds, and it separates dehydration from hyponatraemia instantly. Weight loss >2% = dehydration. Weight gain or no loss = you're overdrinking, and the answer is less water, not more. Anyone running 25–30 km and theorising about hydration should be doing this.
2
Get an ECG during or immediately after a headache
The cheapest high-yield test available, and the one most likely to be skipped. A resting ECG in a quiet clinic three weeks later tells you nothing. If you get a bad one, that's the moment to go in.
3
Ask for a symptom-limited stress test that reproduces the headache
The formal diagnostic standard for cardiac cephalalgia is capturing the headache and the ECG or perfusion change at the same moment. Tell them the goal is to reproduce the symptom, not just to hit a target heart rate.

What to actually say at the appointment

"I get severe headaches brought on by hard running, especially after 25–30 km. They ease with rest. I have no chest pain. I've been calling them migraines and assuming dehydration. I want you to consider whether this could be an anginal equivalent — cardiac cephalalgia — given my calcium score of 91 in the RCA."

The 2021 AHA/ACC Chest Pain Guideline deliberately moved away from "typical vs atypical" language toward classifying symptoms as cardiac, possibly cardiac, or noncardiac — precisely so that presentations other than chest pain get entered into the framework rather than filtered out. Naming it gets it assessed.

Should you stop running? No. The ACC's guidance for athletes with elevated calcium scores is graded progression and symptom vigilance, not restriction — fitness is powerfully protective. The reasonable middle path until the CT angiogram: keep training, but defer the maximal 25–30 km sessions that reliably reproduce the headache, and never train through an episode. Not stopping. Not pushing into the symptom.

Why ezetimibe instead of just more statin

This is the part worth appreciating. In a head-to-head, rosuvastatin 10 + ezetimibe beat rosuvastatin 20 alone — bigger LDL drop (−20.5% vs −13.5%), bigger apoB drop, and fewer adverse events (28.2% vs 40.5%), with no difference in fasting glucose, HbA1c or HOMA-IR.

That matters because statins do modestly raise diabetes risk and you're already prediabetic — and ezetimibe is diabetes-neutral (IMPROVE-IT substudy: HR 1.03, ns). Adding ezetimibe rather than escalating the statin gets you to target with less statin exposure. For a prediabetic endurance runner, that's exactly the right architecture.

Rosuvastatin is also the athlete-preferred statin — it's hydrophilic and penetrates skeletal muscle less than atorvastatin, simvastatin or lovastatin, which carry the highest myopathy risk.

The CK monitoring trap — flag this before anyone tests you. Male athletes have a normal creatine kinase range of 82–1,083 U/L — up to six times the range for inactive people. Post-marathon CK routinely runs into the thousands for days. A CK drawn in the days after a long run is uninterpretable for statin myopathy and will generate a false alarm that could get you taken off a drug you need.

What to do: get a baseline CK during a rest period. Don't test when asymptomatic. When you do test, avoid strenuous exercise for 3–7 days first and tell the lab and the doctor you're an endurance runner.

Practical notes

  • Food: neither drug cares. Rosuvastatin's AUC is unaffected by food; ezetimibe's label says with or without. Take them together, whenever you'll remember.
  • Timing: no evening-dose requirement — rosuvastatin's half-life is ~19 h. Morning is fine.
  • Asian dosing: the FDA label advises starting rosuvastatin at 5 mg in Asian patients — East Asian subjects show 64–84% higher AUC. You may get more effect per mg, and more myopathy risk per mg. Worth knowing if a dose change is discussed.
  • Diabetes monitoring: HbA1c at baseline, ~3 months, then 6-monthly. Expect a possible ~0.1% rise from the statin — anticipated, not a reason to stop. If you did cross into diabetes, the answer is to treat the diabetes, not stop the statin.
  • Muscle red flags: dark/cola urine, severe generalised muscle pain, weakness out of proportion to your training. Those warrant same-day review. The distinguishing sign is proximal weakness — trouble on stairs or rising from a chair — rather than soreness proportionate to the session.
  • Colchicine + rosuvastatin: both can cause myopathy, so the combination gets flagged. The reassuring data: in LoDoCo2, where ~95% were on statins across 30,000+ patient-years, CK above 3× normal occurred in 0.7% vs 0.2% and there were zero rhabdomyolysis cases. Rosuvastatin is also the better partner here — unlike atorvastatin or simvastatin it doesn't compete with colchicine at CYP3A4. With your eGFR above 90 and low-dose colchicine, this is well-supported. Hold colchicine if you're ever prescribed clarithromycin, verapamil, diltiazem or ketoconazole — clarithromycin especially has documented fatalities.
  • Don't stop the statin over the headaches. You had them before starting. A 2025 Lancet meta-analysis of 19 trials and 123,924 people checked 66 labelled side effects and confirmed only four — headache was not among them.
